We submit our manuscript "Measuring and Modeling the Chunk Size of Linear-Recurrence GPU Kernels: Execution Regimes, Measurement Bias and the Limits of Tuning" for consideration as a Regular Paper in the Journal of Parallel and Distributed Computing.

The chunked linear-recurrence kernels behind Mamba, Mamba-2 and Gated DeltaNet expose a chunk size that is the granularity of a blocked parallel scan: it trades on-chip reuse, parallel slack and occupancy against sequential and per-chunk work. The manuscript is a measurement and modeling study of this parameter: how large its effect is, which execution regime exposes it, what a timing can mistake for it, and when a model can replace timing. We study three production kernels (the Mamba-1 CUDA scan, the Mamba-2 SSD pipeline and the Gated DeltaNet pipeline) and a Triton scan that we wrote and use as a stress case, from prefill calls captured from 370M to 2.8B parameter checkpoints up to whole prefill steps captured as CUDA graphs, on Ampere, Hopper and Blackwell GPUs.

Main findings:
1. At the prefill-step level, tuning the chunk beyond the library default is second-order on the one GPU we measured: a held-out shape-keyed table is equivalent to the default within +-0.5% over steps of 256 to 8192 tokens (+-1% for one model under stricter analyses), with a largest gain of 0.23% (95% interval 0.13 to 0.41), because the scan is only 5 to 19% of the step. A wrong static chunk, by contrast, costs up to 15%.
2. The execution regime decides where the chunk is visible: launch-inclusive latency follows the slower of the host's launches and the device, and in single-call CUDA-graph replays the library defaults leave 1.0 to 5.9% of the scan time, mostly on short calls.
3. Buffer placement, and input values under a power cap, can move a single timing more than the chunk does; a tuning cache that times each key once records these as chunk or data effects.
4. The best chunk follows the kernel's specialization signature and the device: adding activation entropy to the shape key changes the held-out selection regret by at most 0.03 points, while a single compiler divisibility bit changes the compiled kernel and its best chunk.
5. A compile-informed model of the stress kernel selects with 0.8 to 7.4% regret with one calibration key left out, yet profiling every key a scheduler can enumerate takes seconds per GPU and costs less than calibrating the model.

We believe the manuscript fits the journal's interest in the performance analysis of parallel applications and in accelerator-based platforms. It discusses related work published in the journal on GPU autotuning and performance prediction: Filipovic et al. (2022) on hardware counters in autotuning, Ernst et al. (2023) on analytical performance estimation during code generation, and Amaris et al. (2023) on analytical and machine-learning predictions of GPU kernel execution times.

Related preprint: the authors also wrote the preprint "COREY: Entropy-Guided Runtime Chunk Scheduling for Selective Scan Kernels" (arXiv:2604.10597), which has not been peer reviewed or published elsewhere. The present manuscript is a new study whose conclusions differ in part: it uses the entropy scheduler of the preprint as the control for data-dependent dispatch. The kernel-level timing results of the preprint came from a harness that split the sequence into independent kernel calls and so mostly measured the number of launches; this manuscript replaces them with measurements that change the chunk inside a single launch that carries the recurrent state, and states this relation in its text.

The manuscript is not under consideration elsewhere, all authors approved the submission, and we have no conflicts of interest. The code, result files and scripts behind every table and figure are in a public Code Ocean capsule. The use of Claude (Anthropic) to debug the existing experiment code and to check the results is declared in the manuscript.
