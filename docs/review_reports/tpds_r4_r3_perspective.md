## 2. Recommendation: **Major Revision**

Decisive reasons, in order:

1. **The production prefill path is not the call that was measured.** Every serving engine the readership runs issues the scan on a *packed, variable-length* batch (cu_seqlens / query_start_loc), i.e. batch 1 in the kernel's eyes with a packed length and sequence boundaries. The paper's batched calls stack same-length prompts cropped to the shortest (Sec. 6.3), and its serving replay "costs packed calls as batch-1 scans of the packed length, since we did not measure a variable-length kernel" (Supp. S9). The headline regrets that make the chunk look important (13.0% and 23.8% at batch 4 and 16, Table 2; "up to 42 and 93%" of calls below break-even for padded budget steps, Sec. 7.8) arise in call shapes that production prefill does not produce. The conclusions would very likely survive a varlen measurement -- which is why this is Major and not Reject -- but the magnitudes a TPDS reader takes away are presently the wrong ones.
2. **The tensor-parallel study is a single-GPU width sweep at TP degrees no one deploys for 1.4B-2.8B models**, on the study's own Triton kernel; the production CUDA scan is width-insensitive (2.2% pooled). The abstract's "a table tuned for one GPU loses up to 63%" is the t=8 shard of a 1.4B model (width 512). Realistic TP shards of deployed hybrids land at widths 2048-4096, which the paper already covers at t=1.
3. **The kernel-level timing regime (eager launch floor, warm L2) differs from the serving regime (CUDA graphs, L2 polluted by the surrounding GEMMs)** and this changes at least one concrete rule the paper hands to implementers: the Mamba-2 work-term model "defaults to the smallest chunk (32...)" when launch-bound, which is the one chunk that is worst once the floor drops.
4. **A cheap, obvious baseline is missing**: pre-profiling the whole bucket grid at startup (15-23 bucket keys per hour in the replay, ~0.03-0.05 s per key for six chunks) costs seconds per GPU SKU and reaches the exact-shape floor, against 8-14 minutes of calibration for a 0.9-3.5% model. The model's real niche (keys that cannot be enumerated in advance, targets one cannot time before deployment) is never argued against that baseline.

All four are fixable without a new research programme: roughly 10-20 single-GPU hours of measurement plus reframing. I would expect to recommend acceptance after a revision that does them.

## 3. Practitioner's assessment of the five questions

### (1) Practical value: could a serving system use this, and what would integration look like?

What a vLLM/SGLang maintainer would do with the paper today:

- **Mamba-2 SSD and Gated DeltaNet layers (Nemotron-H, Falcon-H1, Bamba, Qwen3-Next, Kimi Linear class):** nothing. The paper shows chunk 256 is the oracle for 375 of 397 SSD calls above the launch floor and chunk 64 for 154 of 155 GDN calls (Supp. S6), which is exactly what the shipped code hard-codes (`chunk_size` from the model config; FLA's BT=64). This is a useful *confirmation* and should be stated as such in the abstract; it is currently buried in Sec. 9.
- **Mamba-1 selective-scan layers (Jamba family, the Mamba-1 checkpoints):** the stock length dispatch of the CUDA scan is within 6.6% of the per-call oracle; a shape tree gets 3.0%, the cost model 3.8% (Table 4). So the deployable gain is ~3 points of *scan* latency, and only if the kernel is rebuilt to accept a runtime chunk (the stock kernel fixes it in a template). The paper should say this plainly.
- **Cold keys / new SKUs:** the genuinely new capability is choosing a chunk with zero timing on the target (0.9/3.2/3.5% regret on three GPUs; 3.8% on the H800 with the RTX PRO 6000's parameters and only the H800's compile facts, Supp. S2). That matters for engine-build-time dispatch tables (TensorRT-LLM style) and for fleets where a new SKU appears before anyone has profiled on it.

What integration would require and what is missing for it:

- An explicit **API**: inputs (packed length, number of sequences, shard width, dtype, 16-divisibility, device), the per-architecture compile-facts table (184 launch configurations x 2 alignment variants, compiled offline -- the 353 s first-key compile in Supp. Table "Auto-tuning per shape key" must never run at serve time), the eight parameters, and the dispatch procedure (rank by Eq. (5), profile the top-2 on first sight, cache). The paper has all the pieces but no recipe (Finding 5).
- **How to obtain compile facts for CUDA kernels** (the reference scan uses "a fixed register count of 168 per thread (the count that the assembler reports...)", Supp. S3): say `ptxas -v` / `cuobjdump --dump-resource-usage`, and whether the per-chunk exchange terms must be hand-derived per kernel (they were).
- **Failure modes, in one place**: unaligned variant in batched calls is underestimated (Sec. 7.4); 95th-percentile regret without profiling is 15.4% (RTX 3090) and 14.1% (H800) and 30% at shard widths; "less reliable as a complete auto-tuner of all 184 launch configurations (9% and 23%...)"; the launch-bound default (Finding 4). For a serving SLO the tail matters more than the mean; p95/p99 after top-2 profiling is not reported.
- **When the model is wrong** is answered by the top-2 profile fallback; that hybrid, not the pure model, is the thing to ship, and it should be named as the recommendation.

### (2) The parallel/distributed dimension: enough for TPDS?

Honest answer from the outside: **currently a single-GPU kernel paper with a well-argued serving motivation, not a distributed-systems result.** The three "parallel" pieces are: (i) cross-GPU transfer of the cost model (good, real fleet relevance, but the strawman "a chunk tuned on the RTX 3090 loses 48.8% on the RTX PRO 6000" is something no fleet does); (ii) TP widths timed on one GPU with synthetic inputs (Sec. 7.5, Supp. S5), at t=8 of a 1.4B model; (iii) a simulated prefill scheduler over public traces (Supp. S9) that counts keys and calls but never produces a TTFT or a throughput. Sequence-parallel state exchange is not measured (Sec. 9). No multi-GPU job is run.

**The one experiment I would ask for, with cost.** Replay one hour of the Azure conversation trace (already in hand) through a real engine -- vLLM supports `MambaForCausalLM`, so `state-spaces/mamba-2.8b-hf` fits a single 24-48 GB GPU -- with chunked prefill on (token budget 2048) and three dispatch policies in the engine's selective-scan fork: stock length dispatch, cost model with top-2 first-sight profiling, and a pre-tuned exact-key table. Report TTFT p50/p99, time-per-output-token and tokens/s at prefill loads 0.2/0.5/0.85. Then repeat once at **TP=2 on two GPUs** to confirm that the per-device scan shape is the d/t width the paper assumes and that layer-level time, including the all-reduce, moves as the kernel-level model predicts. Cost: the runtime-chunk kernel exists; porting the patch to the engine's kernel copy is ~2 engineer-days; GPU time ~4-6 single-GPU hours plus ~2 hours on 2 GPUs, i.e. roughly 8-10 GPU-hours total. A null result ("the stock rule suffices end to end; the model matters only for cold keys and new SKUs") is still the right thing to publish and would make the paper far more credible to this readership than a 13th kernel-level table.

A cheaper companion (Finding 2): extend the width sweep to the unsharded widths of deployed hybrids (d_inner 8192 and 16384) and their t=2/4/8 shards, ~2 single-GPU hours, so that the TP claim is about shapes that exist in production.

### (3) Generality beyond the Mamba-1 selective scan

- **Mamba-2 SSD:** the paper's own data say the optimum is fixed at 256 above the launch floor at 370M, 1.3B and 2.7B widths; the cost model's role is nil and the work-term model places the optimum "at about 350 ... and 200", which is "next to" 256 but would select 512 or 128 if taken literally. The generality claim for the *cost model* is Mamba-1 only; the title and abstract should not suggest otherwise.
- **Gated DeltaNet:** chunk limited to 16/32/64 by the library; 64 is always best. Nothing to dispatch.
- **Reference CUDA scan:** chunk and thread count co-vary (32 to 128 threads), so "chunk sensitivity" there is partly launch-geometry sensitivity. The adapted model fits (R^2 0.96) but the exercise is one kernel more, hand-adapted.
- **Larger checkpoints / data-centre GPU:** 1.4B and 2.8B Mamba-1 on an RTX 3090; H800 PCIe for Mamba-1 only. A fleet is H100/H200/B200 SXM; the RTX PRO 6000 (sm_120) is a workstation Blackwell part. One Hopper PCIe card is acceptable evidence of transfer but should not be described as covering "data-centre GPUs" in the plural.
- **The no-gain bound** is general for any data-oblivious kernel and is the one piece that transfers to everything; the information-side evidence (shape + entropy = shape; conditional MI 0.046 bits vs 1.23 for log-length) is the strongest result in the paper.

### (4) Assumptions worth challenging

- **Explicit: "Runtimes tune it offline per shape" (abstract); "In practice it is fixed per model and device by offline tuning" (Sec. 1).** Not how shipped runtimes treat the chunk. SSD's chunk is a model-config constant, FLA's is a compile-time constant, the Mamba-1 CUDA scan dispatches by a length rule in a template. The paper's own data explain why: for the production kernels there is nothing to tune. The paper is better framed as "the Mamba-1 scan is where a shape-dependent optimum exists; here is what the stock rule leaves, and here is why data cannot add anything".
- **Implicit: the decision costs a host synchronization.** No serving engineer would implement it that way. The runtime-chunk kernel the authors built could read its chunk from a device scalar written by the producing kernel's epilogue, or a CUDA-graph conditional node could pick the variant, at near-zero critical-path cost. Sec. 4 concedes this ("we do not measure such implementations") -- so the headline "below one host synchronization" is a statement about one implementation class, and the exclusion really rests on the information side, which the paper proves well. Lead with that.
- **Implicit: prefill is where the chunk matters, and prefill is what matters.** Decode uses the step-update kernel (no chunk, Supp. S1.1), and in chat workloads decode dominates GPU time; under chunked prefill the scan sees packed slices of at most T tokens, batch 1 -- precisely the regime where a single chunk already loses only 1.7-5.3% (Table 2 batch 1; Table 6). The end-to-end ceiling of *any* chunk policy under the replayed traces is never stated (Finding 8).
- **Implicit: the kernel-level timing regime is the serving regime.** Warm L2 between repeats, eager launch floor of 56-90 us (Sec. 6.4), and a Mamba-2 pipeline whose floor halves without the Python launch cost (Supp. S7). In serving, the scan sits between an in-projection GEMM and an out-projection GEMM that evict L2, and decode (and increasingly prefill) runs under CUDA graphs.
- **Paradigmatic: per-call latency is the objective.** For serving, the objectives are p99 TTFT and throughput under an SLO; a policy with 0.9% mean regret and a 15-30% tail is a different product from one with 3% mean and a 5% tail. The paper reports means first and tails sometimes.

### (5) Broader implications, reproducibility, readability

- **Implications.** The paper will stop people from building activation-driven kernel dispatch for data-oblivious kernels, which is worth doing once and well; this paper does it well. It will not change what vLLM/SGLang ship for SSD/GDN (and should say so). Its cost-model path is attractive for engine-build-time tables and heterogeneous fleets.
- **Reproducibility.** Code and data via a Code Ocean capsule; the supplement states versions, drivers, warmups and repeats per table and flags legacy measurements explicitly -- unusually good. Two gaps for a practitioner: the Triton kernel "written for this study" is the main subject and its relation to anything deployable (a drop-in for the mamba_ssm scan? numerics in bf16?) is not stated; clocks are unlocked on cloud instances and no hardware counters were available, which the paper admits but which a fleet operator would want replicated once on a locked, owned node.
- **Readability.** The Introduction, Sec. 4 and Sec. 9 are very good. Secs. 7.4-7.5 and 7.8 are results logs. A systems reader needs: one figure of regret versus calibration budget (none / top-2 / top-3 / grid profile / full tune) per GPU, one recipe box, and the per-GPU numeric detail moved to the supplement, which already holds it.

## 5. Strengths

- **Measurement hygiene a practitioner can trust.** The split-call pitfall (Sec. 6.1), recurrence-preserving timing with output checks, the placement and cold-L2 interventions, TOST equivalence bounds, prompt-grouped cross-validation, and two timing conventions on three GPUs are well beyond what this venue usually sees for kernel studies. The supplement's explicit "legacy, not used for any claim" labelling is exemplary.
- **A negative result that is worth having.** Activation-driven kernel dispatch is a tempting idea; the paper kills it for data-oblivious kernels with a general argument and the right measurements (shape + statistic = shape; conditional MI; a routed end-to-end study with controls). This will save other teams from building it.
- **The compile-informed model is a genuinely useful cold-key default**, and the transfer with compile facts only (3.8% on the H800 with another GPU's parameters) is exactly the property a heterogeneous fleet wants. The top-2 profiling hybrid is a pragmatic, shippable recipe.
- **The right serving questions are asked**: key churn under continuous batching and chunked prefill, break-even per key, public traces, compiler-specialization bits in the cache key (the 16-divisibility finding is a real, reusable lesson).
- **Honest Limitations section** that already names most of what I raise; the revision is mostly a matter of measuring two of those gaps and moving the honesty into the abstract.

## 6. Pointers from the serving side (for the authors' convenience)

- CUDA Graph conditional nodes (NVIDIA CUDA Programming Guide, 12.4+): device-side variant selection without a host round trip -- the mechanism that makes Finding 6's zero-sync measurement a one-afternoon job.
- Lieber et al., "Jamba: A Hybrid Transformer-Mamba Language Model" (2024): the production anchor for the Mamba-1 selective scan; its per-device inner width under TP=8 is the realistic shard width for Finding 2.
- Qwen3-Next technical report (2025) and the already-cited Kimi Linear: production anchors for Gated DeltaNet at chunk 64.
- Dean and Barroso, "The Tail at Scale" (CACM 2013): the framing for why p95/p99 regret, not the mean, is the quantity to report for a dispatch policy under an SLO.
- The vLLM Mamba path (selective-scan fork with query_start_loc; `mamba_chunk_scan_combined` with cu_seqlens/seq_idx) as the definition of the call shape that Finding 1 asks to measure.
