# Summary

The paper investigates dynamic chunk scheduling for memory-bound selective scan kernels in Selective State Space Models (SSMs). The authors introduce COREY, a runtime scheduler that utilizes fixed-bin activation entropy to dynamically select chunk sizes on the fly. Framed as a concept-and-feasibility study, the paper rigorously evaluates this mechanism across prototype cost models, kernel-level timings, and end-to-end routed ablations. Ultimately, the paper presents a highly informative negative result: while the dynamic scheduler can preserve exact bitwise output quality and operate with low inline overhead, the irreducible computational cost of calculating the entropy statistic at runtime outweighs the benefits. A highly tuned static chunk size, or a simple sequence-length rule, outperforms the entropy-guided approach on current Mamba workloads.

# Strengths

* The paper tackles a highly relevant problem (memory bandwidth bottlenecks in SSM inference) with a novel approach, representing the first systems-level exploration of using a dynamic, data-dependent information-theoretic metric (activation entropy) to schedule GPU kernel chunk sizes on the fly.

* The submission provides a highly significant and rigorous "clean negative result." By demonstrating that the irreducible overhead of runtime statistic computation prevents dynamic scheduling from outperforming a static oracle, the paper acts as a valuable diagnostic scaffold that will save the systems community from pursuing unproductive optimization paths.

* The empirical verification that dynamic runtime chunk routing preserves exact bitwise output quality (zero metric deltas and identical perplexity) is a major systems achievement, proving that adaptive routing in SSMs can be deployed without introducing numerical drift or requiring costly re-tuning.

* The inclusion of the empirically falsified Hadamard pre-rotation theory provides a valuable mechanistic insight, formally establishing the distributional prerequisites for entropy growth and explaining why the technique fails to amplify entropy on current approximately Gaussian Mamba activations.



# Weaknesses

* Please consider updating the chunk-size selection formula (Equation 2) to accurately reflect the implementation. The equation currently uses a floor function, which mathematically evaluates to 256 for standard-normal inputs (r=0.83). However, the text (Line 102) and Table 1 state that r=0.83 selects a chunk of 512, suggesting a rounding function was actually used. A similar clarification is needed for the Triton integration formula in Appendix B.5, which evaluates to 448 rather than the claimed 512 for r=0.83.

* The authors might want to clarify the overhead reduction claims regarding the guarded fallback policy. Table 7 shows the absolute latency of the guarded policy (932.86 ms) is virtually identical to the unguarded policy (932.69 ms). The reported reduction to +2-3% overhead seems to arise from comparing against a slower Static-512 baseline from a separate confirmatory run rather than an absolute latency improvement.

* It would be beneficial to ensure compute parity in Table 15. Currently, the table compares policy_corey latencies (measured with explicit warmup) against policy_off and policy_static latencies (measured without warmup, including JIT compilation overhead), which skews the relative performance comparison.

* Please consider correcting the logical connective in the informal statement of Theorem 2 (Section 4). It asserts strict inequality 'unless B is a permutation and p is uniform,' but this should logically be an 'or' condition to align with the formal proof provided in Appendix C.1.

* The authors should consider revising the mechanistic explanation for the entropy decrease after Hadamard rotation (Section 4). Stating that Gaussianity is preserved does not mathematically explain the observed 1.4 nat drop; if the shape were perfectly preserved, discrete entropy would remain roughly unchanged. The drop is likely an artifact of dynamic histogram binning interacting with a non-zero mean.

* Please review the surrogate dispatch cost calculation in Appendix D.1. The cost is calculated as 0.413 ms by dividing the static-64 latency (3.30 ms) by 8 calls. However, Table 20 indicates the static-64 configuration uses 64 calls. Correcting this would yield a true cost of ~0.05 ms, which significantly impacts the surrogate solver latencies reported in Table 37.

### [COREY Methodology and Algorithms] (Pages: 3-4, 25-27)
### 1. Potential Mistakes and Improvements

*   **Contradiction in Chunk-Size Selection Formula (Correctness):** In Section 3.2, Equation (2) defines the selected chunk size using a floor operation: $C_{\text{corey}} = \text{clip}\left( 2^{\lfloor \log_2(C_{\text{min}} + r (C_{\text{max}} - C_{\text{min}})) \rfloor}, C_{\text{min}}, C_{\text{max}} \right)$. Evaluating the formula directly yields $32 + 0.83(480) = 430.4$, and taking the base-2 logarithm gives $\approx 8.749$. Applying the floor function results in 8, which yields $2^8 = 256$, not 512. It appears the implementation utilizes a rounding operation (e.g., `round`) rather than a floor operation ($\lfloor \cdot \rfloor$). Modifying the equation to reflect the implemented math would resolve this contradiction. This same discrepancy also appears in the uniform distribution rows of Table 1.

*   **Calculation Error in Triton Integration Formula (Correctness):** Appendix B.5 introduces a different mapping formula intended for the fused Triton implementation: $T(e) = 32 \cdot \text{round}\left( \frac{64 + \min(e, \log K)/\log K \cdot (512 - 64)}{32} \right)$. For the provided example where $e = 4.60$ and $\log K = 5.55$ ($r \approx 0.83$), this evaluates to $T(e) = 32 \cdot \text{round}(435.84 / 32) = 32 \cdot \text{round}(13.62) = 32 \times 14 = 448$. However, the accompanying text asserts this gives "tile= 512". It is unclear whether the implementation actually uses 448 (which is a valid Triton block size but conflicts with the text) or adds an unstated step to snap to the nearest power of two (512). Clarifying this formula's intended output is necessary for exact reproducibility.

*   **Logical Error in Theorem 2 Informal Statement (Validity):** In Section 4, the informal statement of Theorem 2 asserts that $H(q) \geq H(p)$ with "strict inequality unless B is a permutation and p is uniform." This condition is logically incorrect. If $B$ is a permutation matrix, equality $H(q) = H(p)$ holds for *any* distribution $p$, not just a uniform one. Similarly, if $p$ is uniform, equality holds for any doubly-stochastic $B$. The condition should logically use "or" instead of "and" (i.e., unless $B$ is a permutation or $p$ is uniform), which aligns correctly with the formal proof provided in Appendix C.1.

*   **Ambiguity in Region Entropy $H(R)$ Definition (Clarity):** In Appendix B.1, the utility function for the dynamic programming solver is defined as $U(R) = \alpha H(R) + \beta AI(R) - \gamma M(R)$. While arithmetic intensity $AI(R)$ and memory traffic $M(R)$ are well-defined cumulative metrics for a sequence of operators, $H(R)$ represents entropy, which is a property of a single tensor. The text does not clarify whether $H(R)$ for a multi-operator fusion region $R$ is evaluated on the region's input tensor, its output tensor, or an aggregate of internal activations. This missing detail limits the reproducibility of the DP solver's cost model.

### [Theoretical Properties and Proofs] (Pages: 4-5, 27-30)
### 1. Potential Mistakes and Improvements

*   **Contradictory Mechanistic Explanation for Entropy Decrease (Section 4 & Appendix C.1):** The manuscript reports an empirical entropy decrease ($\Delta H = -1.40 \pm 0.37$ nats) after applying the Hadamard transform to real Mamba checkpoint activations. The provided mechanistic explanation states that "the measured activations are approximately Gaussian, and orthogonal transforms preserve Gaussianity, so no histogram spreading occurs." This explanation mathematically contradicts the reported decrease. If the distribution's shape were perfectly preserved and evaluated over the same effective dynamic range, the discrete entropy would remain unchanged ($\Delta H \approx 0$). An entropy drop of 1.4 nats indicates that the post-rotation distribution is substantially more concentrated relative to the histogram bins. A mathematically consistent explanation for this artifact involves the interaction between the activation's DC component (non-zero mean) and the dynamic histogram binning (which relies on a min/max pass, Line 370). The Hadamard transform concentrates a non-zero mean into a single coordinate ($z_1 \approx \sqrt{d}\mu$). This outlier expands the tensor's $[\min, \max]$ range, which proportionally increases the fixed-bin width. This range expansion compresses the remaining zero-mean coordinates into a smaller fraction of the available bins, explaining why the measured discrete entropy drops. The explanation should be revised to account for the observed $\Delta H$.

*   **Logical Connective Error in Theorem 2 Informal Statement (Section 4):** The informal statement of Theorem 2 asserts that $H(q) \ge H(p)$ holds "with strict inequality unless $B$ is a permutation and $p$ is uniform." This logical formulation implies that if $B$ is a permutation matrix but $p$ is non-uniform, the strict inequality must hold. However, a permutation matrix merely reorders the elements of $p$, resulting in exactly $H(q) = H(p)$ for any $p$. The condition should be updated to use the disjunction "or" (e.g., "unless $B$ is a permutation or $p$ is uniform") to align with the formally correct conditions detailed in Theorem 3 (Appendix C.1).

*   **Exact Verification of Majorization vs. Sinkhorn Approximation (Appendix C.1):** To empirically test the doubly-stochastic mixing assumption $q = Bp$, the text applies Sinkhorn normalization to find an approximate doubly-stochastic matrix $B$ and evaluates the residual $\|q - Bp\|_1$ (Lines 825–834). Verifying whether a probability vector $q$ can be expressed as a doubly-stochastic mixing of $p$ is mathematically equivalent to checking if $q$ is majorized by $p$ ($q \prec p$), according to the Hardy-Littlewood-Pólya theorem. This condition can be evaluated exactly and computationally efficiently by sorting both histograms in descending order and checking if the prefix sums of $q$ are bounded by the prefix sums of $p$. Utilizing this exact theoretical test would remove the approximation error of the Sinkhorn fit and definitively establish whether the majorization condition holds, addressing the theoretical gap mentioned in Lines 835–842.

### 2. Minor Corrections and Typos

*   **Theorem Numbering Consistency:** The doubly-stochastic entropy majorization theorem is labeled "Theorem 2" in Section 4, but is restated and proven as "Theorem 3" in Appendix C.1. Furthermore, the subsequent quantization bound is labeled "Theorem 4," despite there being no third theorem in the main text.

*   **Undefined Random Vector in Theorem 4 (Appendix C.3):** The theorem introduces $x$ as a deterministic vector to establish the geometric bound $\|z\|_\infty \le \|x\|_1 / \sqrt{d}$, but concludes with a probabilistic inequality $\Pr(\|z\|_\infty > T) \le \Pr(\|x\|_1 > T \sqrt{d})$. To formally support the probability measure, $x$ should be explicitly defined as a random vector in the theorem premise.

*   **Theorem 4 Terminology (Appendix C.3):** The theorem is titled "Variance Preservation," but the proof establishes the preservation of the $\ell_2$ norm ($\|z\|_2 = \|x\|_2$). Preserving the $\ell_2$ norm corresponds to variance preservation only if the input vector $x$ has a mean of zero; otherwise, it is norm or energy preservation.

*   **Undefined Variable in Theorem 4 Proof (Appendix C.3):** The variable $s_{ji}$ appears in the sum $|z_j| = |\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}} \sum_{i=1}^d s_{ji} x_i|$ (Line 869).
